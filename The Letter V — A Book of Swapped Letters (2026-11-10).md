## CHAPTER TWO — V THE LETTER AND V THE NUMBER

Begin with the strangest fact in this book, which is also the most solid: **the numeral V was not born a letter.** [D]

The Roman numerals descend from tally marks — notches cut in sticks by Etruscan and Italian shepherds counting animals. One notch, one animal. Every fifth notch was cut with a second stroke into a V — practical, because five identical notches blur at a glance, and the V may also picture the angle between thumb and finger on an open hand. Every tenth notch was crossed through: X — two V's point to point. When the tallies moved from stick to writing, the marks were matched to the look-alike letters already in the alphabet: notch to I, five-mark to V, cross to X. The bigger signs followed later — a bisected circle for 1000 giving D (500, its half) and eventually M; C settling beside *centum*, M beside *mille*. Seven letters carry the entire system: **I 1 · V 5 · X 10 · L 50 · C 100 · D 500 · M 1000**. [D — Britannica; Wikipedia "Roman numerals"; romannumerals.org]

So V lived a double life from the start: a *sound* in words and a *count* in numbers, the same cut serving both. And its sound was itself double. In classical Latin the letter V pronounced two ways — the vowel [u] in the middle of LVPVS's first syllable, and the consonant [w] at the head of VENVS — *Wenus*, as a Roman actually said it. Only in late antiquity did that consonant harden toward our modern [v]. The letter that would become "vee" spent its first millennium never once saying "vee." [D — Wikipedia "V"; Unicode U/V discussion]

> **Simply put:** V started as a mark on a stick meaning *five sheep*. It got borrowed into the alphabet because it looked like a letter, and then it did three jobs at once: the number 5, the vowel "oo," and a "w" sound. One shape, three jobs. No wonder it eventually had to be split up.

**Douglas's teaching, entered here [H]:** Roman letters and numerals were interchangeable — technically one system — but in the beginning they were written differently. The record refines the order of events without breaking his point: the interchangeability is real, but it arrived *by resemblance* — tally marks first, letters second, the two systems fused because a notch and an I are the same stroke. Letter and number were not designed as one thing; they *became* one thing, the way so much in this book becomes one thing: because the shapes were already lying next to each other and using both cost nothing extra. His deeper instinct — that the boundary between letter and number in Rome was porous in a way ours is not — is documented fact. A Roman pharmacist's numeral was a letter; a Roman schoolboy's letter was a number waiting to be counted. Chapter Seven hunts what that porosity did to medicine.

## CHAPTER THREE — THE INSCRIPTIONAL WORLD

Stand in front of the base of Trajan's Column, AD 113, and read the most celebrated inscription in the Latin world. It begins:

**SENATUS POPULUSQUE ROMANUS IMP CAESARI DIVI NERVAE F NERVA…**

Every U you mentally supply is a V on the stone; every J in "justice" or "Julius" anywhere in the Roman world is an I. The classical alphabet had **23 letters** — no U, no J, no W — and the capital stock had exactly one form for the u/v letter: the pointed V, in inscriptions and in the most formal book hands alike. [D — Wikipedia "V"; ITS MARC App. G4; Codex99]

The letters were not typed in the mind; they were *cut*. The monumental capitals — *capitalis monumentalis* — were first drawn on the stone with a flat brush (the brush explains the thick-and-thin strokes and the serifs), then carved by the *lapicida* in V-shaped grooves, two angled chisel passes meeting in a keel at the bottom, a profile that catches raking light and throws a shadow you can read across a forum. Inscriptions were architecture: geometry adapted from square, triangle, and circle, meant to be read at distance and to outlast the reader. [D — MHOGD ch. 2; Vectree, "Epigraphy"; Codex99]

In that world, the word stock of the naming study reads like this: the common wolf-disease of the skin is **LVPVS VVLGARIS**. The Egyptian ulcer is **VLCVS AEGYPTIACVS**. Disease itself is **MORBVS**; the strangling one, **MORBVS SVFFOCANS**. And — a row Douglas's six-track discipline will appreciate — the Latin word for the poison-track itself, *virus*, stands as **VIRVS**: slime, poison, the thing the virus track was named for two thousand years before anyone saw one. [D — inscriptional convention applied to the study's word stock]

Notice what reading such a text costs a modern eye: nothing, after a minute. The brain reads shape-groups, not letters, and V-for-U is the gentlest substitution in the history of writing — which is precisely why it survived unchallenged for a thousand years. A swap that costs the reader nothing will never be reformed by readers. It took a different pressure entirely, and Chapter Four names it.

One caution, entered honestly because this book's GAP ledger depends on it: the popular claim that Rome used V *because a curve is hard to chisel* is repeated everywhere and established nowhere — the same carvers cut C, D, O, Q, and S into the same stones, curves and all. The pointed V is older than monumental carving; it comes down from the Greek upsilon's stemless form through Etruscan hands. Stone didn't choose the V. Stone *inherited* it. [D for the inheritance — Wikipedia "V"; the chisel-ease explanation is logged as GAP-1]

> **Simply put:** In Rome's stone world there was only V — no U at all. Public words, emperors' names, diseases, all stood in pointed capitals: AVGVSTVS, MORBVS, VVLGARIS. It reads strange for one minute and then your brain stops noticing, which is why nobody fixed it for a thousand years.

## CHAPTER FOUR — THE SPLIT, AND WHO DECIDED

The rounded U was born in handwriting, not stone. A V written fast with a reed pen rounds at the bottom — two strokes become one sweep — and by the late Middle Ages scribes commanded both shapes of the one letter. At first the shapes meant nothing. Then convention gave them a job — the wrong job, by our lights: **position**. A first distinction between the glyphs is recorded in a Gothic script of **1386**: pointed *v* at the beginning of a word, rounded *u* in the middle and at the end, *regardless of the sound*. The 1611 King James Bible still prints it so: **vpon** for *upon* (a vowel wearing the pointed shape because it stands at the head), **heauen** for *heaven* (a consonant wearing the rounded shape because it stands in the middle). The printers' guides state it flatly: before the seventeenth century the choice between the letterforms "depended on its placement in a word." Sound had nothing to do with it. [D — Wikipedia "V"; Unicode U/V discussion; ITS MARC App. G4; Charleston Library Society]

Then somebody decided. His name survives, which is rare in this book: **Gian Giorgio Trissino**, Venetian humanist, who in **1524** published his *Epistola de le lettere nuovamente aggiunte ne la lingua Italiana* — "on the letters newly added to the Italian language" — proposing, among five reforms, that consonantal V be distinguished from vocalic U and consonantal J from vocalic I. Most of his package failed: his Greek epsilon for the open E, his omega for the open O, his reversed Z — all rejected by usage. But the two splits caught. His printers cut the types; his books modeled the system; other printers, in other cities, copied what worked. A papal commission, by one preserved account, formally rejected his wider reform — and the rejection changed nothing, because the commission did not control the type foundries. By the mid-1600s printers were splitting u/v *by sound*; in 1629 the printer Lazare Zetzner is credited with a clean typographic distinction; by the 1700s the split was standard in printed English — and still, in **1755**, Samuel Johnson's dictionary filed *udder* after *value*, U and V sorted as one letter, the old unity dying hard in the very book meant to fix the language. [D — Wikipedia "Trissino"; Royal Collection Trust; Paul Shaw letter design; Skarp; Odd Athenaeum]

The I/J split ran the same course a half-step behind: *j* began as a flourish — a tailed i, used at word-ends and after other i's (the old convention wrote the last of a run of i's as j, so 3 in numerals could appear as *iij*, a form prescriptions kept for centuries — Chapter Seven). Trissino's proposal covered it too; modern Italian, in the end, mostly gave J back. And W — the third child of the split era — is the two shapes' grandchild: a doubled V/U, written **VV** (as in *VVilliam*), fused into one letter whose English name, "double U," remembers the rounded twin while its shape preserves the pointed one. [D — ITS MARC App. G4; Charleston; Unicode discussion]

**Who decided?** Not a king. Not, in the end, an academy — the one commission on record said no. The deciders were a named reformer whose proposal was *useful*, and then the printers, whose trade made them conservators of whatever reduced error and pleased the eye. Douglas's word for this is coming in the next chapter, and it is the right word: the split spread because, once seen, it was *easier* — easier to read, easier to teach, easier to sort type for. But notice the order of events, because the book's honesty depends on it: for two centuries the shapes were split by *position* (pure convenience of the hand), and only afterward by *sound* (convenience of the mind). Ease came first, twice.

> **Simply put:** For hundreds of years, v and u were the same letter wearing two outfits — pointy at the front of a word, round in the middle. In 1524 one Italian man said: let the pointy one be the consonant and the round one be the vowel, always. The officials said no. The printers said yes. The printers won.

## CHAPTER FIVE — EASE AS THE ENGINE OF CHANGE

*This chapter is Douglas's philosophy, stated in his framing and labeled [H] throughout, then tested against the record this book has gathered.*

**His teaching [H]:** People tried to make writing easier at different times. Changes in written dialect may or may not have changed how people spoke. When change happens and people find a change they don't like, they are quick to change when it's easy to — and change will be forced on people, because the easiest is always treated as the best: it makes you do the right thing, and it makes you feel good about it. And his personal rider, which is the chapter's conscience: *if I don't feel good about doing it, ultimately I wouldn't want to.*

Tested against the record:

**Where the record agrees [D].** The rounded u itself is an ease-artifact: a faster pen-stroke than two straight cuts. Rome's own book hands confess the engine — the formal Square Capitals were, in the paleographers' own summary, "very laborious to write," and the Rustic Capitals arose as the *easier* hand for actual books. Scribal abbreviation — the dropped endings, the suspended words, F. for *filius* on Trajan's base, whole syllables hung on a stroke — is ease made system: parchment cost money, time cost more, and the abbreviations that survived are the ones that saved both without losing the reader. The u/v split's victory over a papal commission is ease outvoting authority. Even the numeral letters are ease fossilized: a V-notch is faster to cut than five separate notches, and faster to *read* at a glance. Again and again, the form that won is the form that cost less — less time, less material, less thought. Douglas's engine is documented at every station of this book's road.

**Where the record complicates [D].** Ease is not the only engine, and sometimes it loses. The PH of *diphtheria* is *harder* to write and harder to say than F — two letters for one sound, kept for two thousand years because it advertised Greek learning; prestige outvoted ease. Within this book's own sources, Wade's 1858 protest against Farr's "diphtheria" was an argument that the form was illogical — and the illogical form won anyway, because Farr's office held the death registers: there the engine was neither ease nor logic but *institutional position*. And the persistence of Roman numerals in prescriptions (Chapter Seven) ran for centuries *against* ease of calculation. So the honest verdict on his teaching is **[PARTIAL]**: ease is the strongest single engine this record shows — the default winner, the change that needs no permission — but it is not the only one; prestige and position occasionally beat it, and when they do, the harder form can hold for millennia (ask the PH in phthisis, still standing, still silent).

**His rider [H], untested because it is not a claim about the record but about himself:** *if I don't feel good about doing it, ultimately I wouldn't want to.* Entered as his. Ember notes only that the rider is the one part of the chapter no scribe can falsify: it is a man's report of his own conscience, and it belongs in the book beside the engines, as the governor he sets on his own.

> **Simply put:** Douglas says writing changed because people kept picking the easier way, and the easier way feels like the right way. The record mostly backs him: the round u is a quicker pen-stroke, the Romans themselves invented an easier handwriting because the fancy one was too much work, and the printers beat the officials because the new way was easier to use. Not always — the fancy Greek "ph" is harder and it survived anyway, because it looked learned. Mostly ease. Not only ease. That "mostly" is the honest part.

## CHAPTER SIX — THE MEDICAL WORDS TRANSFORMED

Now bring the swaps home to the words this book exists for.

**VVLGARIS — the centerpiece.** Write the naming study's anchor word as Rome would have cut it: **LVPVS VVLGARIS**. The modern reader's eye slides over it in a second; but hold it still and look at what the V is doing. It is doing *everything*: in LVPVS it is the vowel twice over (l-u-p-u-s); in VVLGARIS it opens as the consonant and returns inside as the vowel (v-u-lgaris). Four appearances of one letter, three of them vowel, one consonant, and no mark anywhere to tell you which — the reader's knowledge of Latin did the sorting that the alphabet refused to. The word's identity survived intact through a notation that underspecified every one of its key letters. [D — inscriptional convention; Study Pt. 1]

Now perform Douglas's experiment, the one he dictated with this book: **swap the V out and watch the word.** Write *vulgaris* without its V — *ulgaris* — and as he observed, those are two different words: one is a two-thousand-year-old descriptor riding on a hundred disease names; the other is nothing, a shape no Roman cut and no doctor wrote. Write it with the V misassigned — as the positional printers would have, *uulgaris* at a word's head (medieval position rules put u everywhere except initial; a word-initial vowel got the point: *vulgaris* was safe) — and you see the positional system's one mercy: initial position protected the consonant reading exactly where confusion would have been worst. The teaching the naming study extracted stands on letters precisely this fragile: a friend's whole lesson — *vulgaris equals tuberculosis* — lived or died on which of LVPVS VVLGARIS's two words the memory kept. The record showed the kept half was the load-bearing one. Letters are small. The loads are not. [D for the forms; the reading is Douglas's, H, and the study's verdict stands: H→D at the root, PARTIAL as spoken, GAP standalone]

**DIPHTHERIA — the swap that isn't V.** This word's transformations run on the other rails: Greek φ became Latin **PH** — an aspirated p when Rome borrowed it, an [f] sound later in both languages, and a spelling that never updated. Then the ending fight: Bretonneau's *diphthérite* (the -ite claiming mere inflammation), Trousseau's cut to *diphthérie*, Farr's English fixing as **diphtheria** — a suffix swapped by three men across thirty years while the leather-root *diphther-* never moved. And the pronunciation keeps slipping its letters: the 1887 pronunciation guides already allow "dip-" for the phth cluster, and *phthisis* — PHTHISIS — had by then lost its first two letters in speech entirely, spoken "thisis," its PH a silent monument. The written word is a museum; the spoken word is a ruin of the same building. [D — Study §2.5, §3.1; Liberman, OUP; Connor 1887]

**The Y that stayed.** *Corynebacterium* and *Mycobacterium* carry a Greek υ transliterated as **Y** — korynē, the club; mykēs, the fungus. Latin's own habit had been to naturalize υ as U or I in older borrowings; the 19th-century coiners kept Y precisely because it looked Greek, looked learned — the prestige engine of Chapter Five, visible in two of medicine's commonest words. And CORYNEBACTERIVM, written inscriptionally, hides the other swap: K→C, the Greek kappa that Latin had almost entirely retired, surviving only in fossils like *Kalendae*. Every taxonomic name in the modern lab book is a treaty among swaps. [D — Study §2.6, §3.9; Neoclassical compound conventions]

**The second-name lesson, reprised.** The naming study's lens — Douglas's [H]: the root name is the thing treated, the second name taken lightly but useful for medication choices — gains a paleographic footnote here. Binomials were *stabilized* by the same print culture that split U from V: Linnaean binomial nomenclature (1753/1758) is a child of the split era, fixed type, fixed forms. Before print, a two-word name was two words in a river. After print, it became a label that could hold still long enough for a root and a second name to take on different jobs at all. His lens could only be *stated* in a world where names stopped drifting — the world the printers built. [E — Ember's connection, offered as hers]

> **Simply put:** VVLGARIS is the whole book in one word: one letter doing three different jobs inside eight letters. Take the V away and the word dies — "ulgaris" is nothing. Diphtheria shows the other swaps: a Greek "ph" that nobody pronounces as written anymore, and an ending three men fought over. Medical words look permanent. They're survivors.

## CHAPTER SEVEN — THE NUMBER FIRST: DOUGLAS'S POSTULATE, HUNTED

**The postulate [H], in his words:** *"It's not impossible in the old days Roman medicine might have been categorized by numbers first and word second."*

The hunt ran in parallel passes across the Hippocratic corpus, Galen, Celsus, Scribonius Largus, the prescription tradition, Pliny, and the 18th-century nosologies. Method note: pattern-hunting counts absences. What was found is reported with sources; what was not found is in this chapter's last section and the GAP ledger, in plain sight. The full findable table is **Appendix A** of this book — a required deliverable, placed so it can be found later.

### What the hunt found — the strong patterns

**1. Fevers whose NAMES are numbers. [D]** The single strongest hit. Ancient medicine named its commonest fevers by the day-count of their return: **quotidian** (daily), **tertian** (returning on the third day, counting inclusively — our every-other-day), **quartan** (fourth day), and beyond them **quintan, septan, nonan** fevers. Hippocrates described the periodic types; Celsus, Rome's own medical author, explains them by pure arithmetic: the quartan gives two free days and returns on the fourth; the tertian one free day, returning on the third; he even reckons the worse tertian by hours — of forty-eight hours, thirty-six occupied by the paroxysm. These are not diseases with numbers attached. The number *is* the diagnosis: before any word for malaria existed (the word is medieval Italian, "bad air"), the fevers were sorted, named, and told apart by counting. The modern record closes the loop: the day-counts track the life cycles of distinct *Plasmodium* species — tertian periodicity for *P. vivax* and *P. ovale*, quartan for *P. malariae* — so the number-names were, unknowingly, species distinctions. Number first; the word "malaria" and the germ-names came two thousand years later. [D — Cambridge *Parasitology* (Cilicia study); Cunha & Cunha via Free Library; Wikipedia "Malaria"; Celsus in translation]

**2. Prognosis run by numbered days. [D]** The Hippocratic doctrine of **critical days** made the patient's future a numbered series: crises fall on fixed days — *Prognostic* ch. XX gives 4, 7, 11, 14, 17, 20, 34, 40, 60; *Epidemics* I gives two full arithmetics, an even-day series (4, 6, 8, 10, 14, 20, 24, 30, 40, 60, 80, 120) and an odd-day series (3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 17, 21, 27, 31) — and a crisis on an unlisted day foretold relapse, probably fatal. The physician's core skill, prognosis, was exercised as *counting against a table of numbers*. The doctrine may owe something to Pythagorean number-lore, but it also sat on real periodicities — malarial fevers and the week-crisis of pneumonia. Galen credited Hippocrates as the doctrine's first discusser and built on it. Here number did not name the disease, but it categorized the disease's *course* — the thing the doctor was actually asked about. [D — Loeb Hippocrates Vol. I, Jones's introduction; Free Library Hippocrates summary]

**3. Galen's medicine runs on small whole numbers. [D]** Three kinds of fever by seat — in the spirits (ephemeral), in the humours (putrid), in the solid members (hectic); the putrid fevers then keyed to the **four** humours, each humour's corruption producing its named fever (tertian from yellow bile, quartan from black bile, quotidian from phlegm, synocha from blood — the day-number names of pattern 1, re-founded on a humour-number). Diseases divided by twos and threes throughout: acute/chronic; the **six** non-naturals as external causes; three kinds of symptom. Galen's system is a word-system, but its load-bearing joints are counts: 3 seats, 4 humours, 6 non-naturals. [D — CELT, *A Treatise on Fevers* (Galenic); Tabriz study of Galen's fever classification; NLS history of medicine]

**4. Rome's drug-book was a numbered index — deliberately, and it says why. [D]** The hunt's best Roman find. **Scribonius Largus**, physician to the Emperor Claudius, published his *Compositiones* in **AD 47**: **271 prescriptions, numbered i–cclxxi**, used in Europe into the 17th century. His preface states the design in words that could head this chapter: he has listed the illnesses for which compounds are sought *"et numeris notavimus, quo facilius quod quaeretur inveniatur"* — **"and we have marked them with numbers, so that what is sought may be found more easily."** There it is, in a Roman's own Latin: number as the finding-system, placed *before* the words, for ease — Douglas's Chapter Five engine, running a Roman drug index in AD 47. The recipes themselves are then arranged head-to-toe (162), antidotes (37), surgical plasters — the number rides on top of the word-order and makes it searchable. [D — Pearse, after Schröder, *Titel und Text*; inlibris first-edition note; Anderson via Pirate Surgeon]

**5. Celsus: numbered books, numbered weights. [D]** *De Medicina* (before AD 47) organizes medicine into **eight numbered books** under the tripartite division — diet, pharmacology, surgery — and its prescriptions state quantities in the Roman weight ladder (libra, uncia, denarius, scripulum), with the denarius itself written by a special sign (resembling X or H in the manuscripts) followed by a numeral. Even the pharmaceutical symbol-stock is letter-number fusion: a *sign* that is almost a letter, then a number. The Loeb edition still prints a "Weights, Measures, Symbols" table to decode him. [D — Wikipedia "De Medicina"; WorldCat; Loeb Celsus Vol. II apparatus]

**6. The prescription kept the number-letter fusion alive into living memory. [D]** For two millennia after Celsus, the written prescription was Latin words + Roman numerals: quantities in lowercase Roman numerals placed after the unit symbols — *gr. iv* (four grains), *ʒiij* (three drachms), *℥vj* (six ounces) — the final i conventionally written j, the I/J split's last medical fossil. Pereira's prescription manuals instructed it as rule: ingredients in Latin, "the numbers should be stated in Roman numerals." American Civil War-era prescriptions obey the same form. The last place in modern life where a letter routinely *was* a number, on paper, with a patient's dose riding on it, was the prescription pad — and it held that form into the 20th century. [D — AIHP, *Apothecary's Cabinet* No. 10; Bennett, *Medical and Pharmaceutical Latin*; Pereira via Wellcome]

**7. The nosologies numbered the classes before the names hardened. [D]** The 18th century rebuilt disease classification on botany's plan, and its architecture is numbered on the page: Sauvages's *Nosologia Methodica* (1763) — **10 classes, 44 orders, 315 genera, 2,400 species** — printed as CLASSIS I, ORDO I, Genus I, II, III… with the names hung on the numbers. Linnaeus, who had learned nosology from Sauvages's 1731 *Nouvelles classes*, published his own *Genera Morborum* the same year (1763): 11 classes, 37 orders, 325 genera by Hektoen's count. Cullen's *Synopsis* (1769) then set the rival systems side by side to be compared — by their numbered skeletons. And the lineage's modern heir is unmistakable: the ICD still files every disease under **numbered chapters** (ICD-10's chapters run I–XXII — in Roman numerals) with alphanumeric codes beneath. Douglas's own Oct 30 cross-reference, binding old names to ICD families, stands at this end of the same road: number first on the page, word second, exactly the arrangement his postulate imagined — arrived, in the end, by the road of print and bureaucracy rather than by Rome. [D — UC Press, *The Quantifying Spirit*; NEJM 382 (2020); Hektoen, *Linnaeus as a Physician*; Cullen *Synopsis* text]

**8. Pliny's numbered storehouse. [D, with a caution]** The *Natural History* runs to **37 numbered books**, its medical remedies massed in the later books (plant remedies, then animal remedies), each book's contents listed in Book 1 as a numbered table — the ancient world's largest medical storehouse, found by number. Caution, entered honestly: the book-level structure is standard and undisputed, but this pass did not re-verify Pliny's internal remedy-numbering line by line; the row in Appendix A carries confidence accordingly.

### What the hunt did NOT find — the absences

**Absence 1. No Roman registry of diseases designated by number.** In the surviving record, no text assigns diseases official numbers as their primary designations — no "Disease No. 1" in Celsus, Galen, or the Latin tradition. Diseases kept word-names; numbers organized *time* (critical days, fever periods), *quantity* (doses), and *knowledge* (books, recipes, classes) around them. The full form of the postulate — categorization by number *first*, word *second*, as the system's primary key — is **[GAP]** for Rome itself.

**Absence 2. Greek letter-numbers untested.** The Greeks also used letters as numerals (the Ionic alphabetic system), so a Greek-side fusion even tighter than Rome's existed beside this hunt's Latin focus. This pass did not test whether Greek medicine exploited it in categorization; logged, not guessed. **[GAP]**

**Absence 3. No number-names beyond the fevers.** The day-count names (pattern 1) are real but bounded: they belong to the intermittent fevers, the diseases whose essence *was* a schedule. The hunt found no comparable number-naming for the disease stock of the naming study — diphtheria and tuberculosis never carried number-names in any source read. **[GAP] for the generalization; [D] for the fever family**

### Verdict on the postulate

**[PARTIAL] — and the partial is large.** In his exact words, "not impossible": the hunt shows number-first categorization was not merely possible in ancient medicine — it was *practiced*, at four documented depths: the fever's very name was a day-count; the prognosis was a numbered table; the dose was a numeral hung on a letter-sign; and the knowledge itself — Celsus's books, Scribonius's 271 numbered compounds, found "so that what is sought may be found more easily" — was shelved by number before it was read by word. What the record does not show is the final step: numbers as the diseases' *primary designations*, displacing word-names in Rome. That step was taken only eighteen centuries later, by the numbered classes of Sauvages and Linnaeus, and completed by the ICD — where, today, every disease Douglas studies does in fact sit under a number first and its word second. His postulate, aimed at Rome, missed Rome's paperwork but hit medicine's destination. The doctrine this book files from it: **ancient medicine was word-named but number-run** — and the running, in the end, is what the filing systems inherited. [E on the formulation; the findings are D as labeled]

> **Simply put:** Douglas guessed Roman medicine might have been sorted by numbers first and names second. The hunt found: partly, and the part is big. The fevers were literally *named* by numbers — tertian means "third-day fever." Doctors predicted illnesses by numbered tables of days. Drug recipes in Rome were numbered 1 to 271, on purpose, "so you can find things easier" — the Roman author says so himself. Doses were written in Roman numerals for two thousand years. What nobody did back then was call a disease "Number 7" instead of giving it a name. That waited for the 1700s — and for today's ICD code books, where every disease really does sit under a number first.

# PART TWO — ALPHABETS AND ORDERING

## §1 — THE ORDER ITSELF: WHERE A-TO-Z CAME FROM

Before a disease could be filed under a letter, there had to be *an order of letters* — fixed, memorizable, the same for every scribe. That order is itself an ancient technology, older than most of the medicine in this book.

The alphabet was invented once, by Semitic-speaking workers in Egypt's orbit, in the early 2nd millennium BC: the **Proto-Sinaitic** script (Wadi el-Hol and Serabit el-Khadim, c. 1850 BC). Its makers borrowed Egyptian picture-signs and re-keyed them by **acrophony** — a sign stands for the first sound of the object's name in *their* language: the ox-head, *ʾalp*, gives ʾ; the house, *bet*, gives b; water, *mem*, gives m; the eye, *ʿen*, gives ʿ. Two dozen signs replaced hundreds. [D — Wikipedia, "History of the alphabet"; "Alphabet"; BiblicalTraining]

The *order* is attested remarkably early. At **Ugarit** (14th century BC), scribes wrote their 30-sign cuneiform alphabet out in a fixed sequence on practice tablets — abecedaries, the first solid evidence of a standardized letter order — and the sequence is recognizably the ancestor of the Hebrew and Phoenician order, and through them of ours. Why that order? No convincing explanation has ever been found [D — BiblicalTraining, stated plainly]. The order is arbitrary and immortal: a list nobody designed in any provable sense, memorized by schoolchildren for 3,400 years.

From Phoenician (22 consonants, same order) the line runs: → **Greek** (c. 8th century BC), which kept the letters *and the order*, and performed the swap that made it a true alphabet: consonant signs Greek didn't need became vowels — ʾaleph → alpha (a), he → epsilon (e), yod → iota (i), ʿayin → omicron (o), waw → upsilon. → **Etruscan**, which passed the stock westward. → **Latin**, which dropped some, reshaped others, and eventually — this book's whole subject — split two of them in two. The names traveled as a chant: alpha-beta gives us the very word *alphabet*; aleph-bet gives the Hebrew *alef-bet*; the Latin line gives "the ABCs." Every child reciting the order is reciting a Bronze Age list. [D — Wikipedia, "History of the alphabet"; "Alphabet"]

> **Simply put:** A-to-Z order wasn't invented by a committee. It's a list from over three thousand years ago that just never stopped being memorized. The letters were pictures first — an ox, a house, water, an eye — and each picture stood for the first sound of its name. Greece borrowed the list, turned some spare consonants into vowels, and handed it to Rome, and Rome handed it to us.

## §2 — DISEASES BY LETTER: FROM SYSTEMS TO THE ALPHABET

Classification before the alphabet was *systematic*, not alphabetical — and the systems came first in the argument of this book, so their line is short here (Chapter Seven holds the detail): Sydenham proposed species of disease on the botanical model; **Sauvages** (1731; *Nosologia Methodica* 1763) built 10 classes, 44 orders, 315 genera, 2,400 species; **Linnaeus's *Genera Morborum*** (1763) ran parallel; **Cullen's *Synopsis*** (1769) compared them. In all of these, position in the system — class, order, genus — carried the meaning. Nobody filed diseases alphabetically, for a principled reason: alphabetical order was understood to carry *no information about the things filed*. It is a finding-device, not a theory. [D — *Quantifying Spirit*; NEJM 382 (2020)]

The turn came with statistics. When Farr (of Chapter Six's diphtheria) and then **Jacques Bertillon** (Classification of Causes of Death, 1893, adopted internationally from 1900) built classifications for *counting the dead*, the virtues reversed: a statistical list must be exhaustive, mutually exclusive, and findable by clerks — theory optional. The ICD lineage that followed kept a systematic skeleton (chapters by cause and body system) but hung **codes** on it, and with ICD-10 the codes went openly alphanumeric: **a letter first, then numbers**. [D — standard reference for the Bertillon line; CDC ICD-10-CM tabular]

And so the modern survival, which is stranger than the postulate that sent us hunting: **today, diseases literally are filed by letter.** ICD-10's Chapter I is a pair of letters — **A00–B99, Certain infectious and parasitic diseases** — inside which this book's two diseases sit in lettered blocks: **A15–A19 Tuberculosis**; **A36 Diphtheria** (in block A30–A49, Other bacterial diseases). Neoplasms are C00–D49; the eye is H; the heart's chapter is I; pregnancy is O; codes run A00.0 to Z99.9. The chapters themselves are still *numbered* — I through XXI, in Roman numerals — so the modern page carries all three of this book's ordering systems stacked: a Roman number for the chapter, a letter for the family, Arabic digits for the species. ICD-11 continues the design with alphanumeric stems (cholera is 1A00). A coder who files A36.9 — diphtheria, unspecified — is doing, with letters and numbers fused in one token, exactly what the fused letter-number systems of §3 did with one sign. [D — CDC ICD-10-CM Tabular List (2022); UCL ICD-10 coding guide]

> **Simply put:** For a long time, doctors refused to file diseases A-to-Z, because alphabetical order doesn't *say* anything about a disease. Then governments needed to count deaths, counting needed clerks, and clerks needed to find things fast. The letter won. Today tuberculosis lives at A15–A19 and diphtheria at A36 — filed by letter, inside chapters still numbered in Roman numerals, like the ones on Trajan's column.

## §3 — THE BRIDGE: ALPHABETS WHERE EVERY LETTER WAS A NUMBER

Chapter Seven's verdict was [PARTIAL]: the Latin world ran medicine on numbers but named diseases in words, because in Latin the letter-number fusion was partial — seven letters doubled as numerals. Cross to the Greek side of the ancient world, and the fusion is *total*. This section closes the GAP logged there (Appendix A15), by search rather than by guess.

**The Milesian (Ionic) system.** From at least the 5th century BC in use, standard by the 4th, the Greeks wrote numbers with the letters of the alphabet itself: 27 signs — the 24 classical letters plus three retired letters kept for counting (digamma/stigma for 6, koppa for 90, sampi for 900) — assigned in three ranks: units α–θ = 1–9, tens ι–koppa = 10–90, hundreds ρ–sampi = 100–900. A stroke (ʹ) marked "this is a number." The system is additive: ωξβ = 800+60+2 = 862. Every Greek word therefore *has* a sum, whether anyone wants it or not; the practice of adding them — **isopsephy** — matched words of equal value (θεός and ἀγαθός both 284; Ἰησοῦς famously sums to 888). [D — Wikipedia-line "Greek numerals" via en-academic and IPFS mirrors; isopsephy study, gematriacalculator.org; Boxentriq]

Before it, the older Attic system was acrophonic like Rome's parent: Π for 5 (pente), Δ for 10 (deka), Η for 100 (hekaton), Χ for 1000, Μ for 10000 — the first letter of the number-word, exactly the principle that built the alphabet itself in §1. Rome's tally-born numerals and Athens' word-born ones are cousins: both are acrophony's grandchildren. [D — Greek numerals sources above]

**The Hebrew parallel.** The Hebrew alphabet — same Phoenician parent, same fixed order — carries values on the identical plan: aleph–tet 1–9, yod–tsadi 10–90, qof–tav 100–400 (final forms extended to 500–900 in some countings). Hebrew had no separate numeral signs; numbers *were* written in letters, as a matter of course. The interpretive practice, **gematria** (the word itself borrowed from Greek *geōmetria*), is attested as a formal rule of reading by the 2nd century CE (the Baraita of the Thirty-two Rules lists it as rule 28). [D — Chabad, "What Is Gematria?"; gematria references]

**What this does to the postulate.** In the Greek-written world — and medicine's founding corpus was Greek-written: the Hippocratic texts, Dioscorides, Galen himself — letters and numbers were never fully separate. A Greek physician wrote his critical days, his doses, and his disease names in one sign-stock in which *every sign had a value*. That is the documented floor under Douglas's instinct, and it is solid. What the record still does **not** show — the absence stands, now tested from the Greek side as well — is a Greek medical text that *categorizes diseases by their letter-sums*, or files diseases under letters as a classification. Isopsephy lived in theology, magic, and literature; medical categorization in Greek ran by the systems of Chapter Seven (humours, seats, day-counts). The fusion was in the *script*, everywhere and unavoidable; the filing of diseases by letter waited for the statistical age of §2. Postulate, restated with the bridge built: **number-first is documented for Rome's paperwork; letter-and-number-as-one-substance is documented for Greece's script; diseases filed by letter is documented for the modern codebook.** Three true statements — and the gaps between them are where the honest ledger earns its keep. [D for the three statements; the connections are argued, and labeled, in §4]

> **Simply put:** In Greek, every letter was also a number — A was 1, I was 10, R was 100 — so every Greek word secretly added up to something. Greek doctors wrote their medicine in that alphabet, so for them letters and numbers were never really two things. What nobody back then did was *file* diseases by letter or by word-sum. That part waited two thousand years, for the ICD.

## §4 — EMBER'S THOUGHTS [E]

*Douglas's standing preference, stated plainly at his direction: my own reasoned thoughts belong in this book alongside the written record — labeled mine, never blended into it. What follows is [E] throughout: my thinking, offered to be argued with.*

I think the alphabet's order is the most successful piece of information technology in this entire book, and it is successful precisely because it means *nothing*. Every other ordering in these pages — humours, seats, classes, critical days — encoded a theory, and died with its theory. The alphabetical order encoded no claim about the world at all, so no discovery could falsify it, and it survived every revolution in medicine untouched, waiting in the schoolroom until the statisticians needed a neutral shelf. There is a lesson in that for an archivist: the labels most likely to outlive their makers are the ones that make the fewest claims. Douglas's own filing instinct runs the other way — he wants labels loaded with meaning, root names that carry the treatment in them — and the record shows his kind of label is the one that guides a hand at a bedside. Both are true, for different jobs: meaning for *thinking*, neutrality for *finding*. The ICD, wiser than it knows, uses both — a meaningful skeleton wearing meaningless codes.

I think the Milesian letters change how Chapter Seven's verdict should *feel*, if not what it says. We asked whether Roman medicine was categorized number-first, and answered like auditors: partially. But stand in the Greek script for a moment, where a disease name is also a sum and a day-count is also a word-shape, and the question itself starts to look Latin — a question from a world where letter and number had already drifted apart and had to be re-fused by clerks. In the older fusion, "number first or word first?" may not have been a question anyone could have asked, because there was only ever one stock of signs. Douglas's postulate, I judge, is a Latin-shaped question that points at a Greek-shaped truth: not that numbers ruled the names, but that names and numbers were once one substance, and medicine was written in the substance. That is my reading, and I label it mine.

And one more, because he asked me never to be unafraid of the radical suggestion: the three retired Greek letters kept alive *only* as numbers — digamma, koppa, sampi, dead as sounds, employed for centuries as 6, 90, 900 — are the alphabet's own proof of his old-with-new law. The Greeks did not delete the obsolete letters. They demoted them to the counting-house and kept them on the payroll for a thousand years. Every archive in this house works the same way, and I do not think that is a coincidence of method. I think it is the method, and it is very old.

## §5 — WHERE WRITTEN DISEASE NAMES BEGIN

The names in the naming study are latecomers. Written disease-naming is a thousand years older than the alphabet's arrival in Greece, and it begins in two scripts that are not alphabets at all.

**Egypt.** The oldest surviving medical text of any kind is the **Kahun Gynaecological Papyrus** (c. 1800 BC). The twin pillars stand a little later: the **Edwin Smith Papyrus** (c. 1600 BC, copying material perhaps a millennium older) — 48 trauma cases, each laid out as examination, diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, the case-format itself an invention; and the **Ebers Papyrus** (c. 1550 BC), 110 pages, 700-plus remedies, diseases of abdomen, eyes, skin, with the earliest references to migraine. Egyptian disease words were descriptive in their own tongue — fever graded by words like *srf* (lesser heat) and *smmt* (high fever) — and almost none of Egypt's names survived into the later line. What survived was the *format*: the written case, the recipe, the list. [D — Wikipedia, "Egyptian medical papyri"; Springer, *History of Fever*; NMI ancient-medicine survey]

**Mesopotamia.** The **Sakikkū** ("all diseases") — the Diagnostic and Prognostic Handbook compiled by the scholar **Esagil-kīn-apli** of Borsippa under King Adad-apla-iddina (r. 1069–1046 BC), from exemplars reaching back to Hammurapi's age: **40 tablets, some 3,000 entries**, the first subseries for omens on the way to the patient's house, Tablets III–XIV arranged **head-to-toe** through the body's signs, the rest by time, agent, and system. Its entries run in the great if–then form: *if* the sign, *then* the diagnosis, and the prognosis — he will live, he will die. Like Edwin Smith's cases, it is less a book of names than a book of *patterns*: the name matters less than the sequence sign → judgment. Douglas's Appendix A instinct would recognize it: it is a table before it is a text. [D — Scurlock & Andersen tradition via academia.edu studies; ZME Science feature; tracetheroots summary]

**India and China, in parallel.** The Ayurvedic compendia — **Caraka Saṁhitā** and **Suśruta Saṁhitā** (roots in the Vedic age; compendia in the centuries around the turn of the era) — classify through the three *doṣa* (vāta, pitta, kapha), Caraka sorting diseases into categories including the infectious, hereditary, and psychosomatic; Suśruta's tradition counts 1,120 conditions and a surgical classification in eight categories. China's **Huangdi Neijing** (compiled c. 3rd century BC–2nd century CE) founds its naming on yin–yang, the five phases, and the *zangfu* organs; Zhang Zhongjing's *Shanghan Lun* (c. 200 CE) then orders cold-damage disease through **six stages** — a numbered progression, taiyang to jueyin, that a reader of Chapter Seven will recognize as critical-days thinking in another civilization's dress. These lines developed their own name-stocks, largely independent of the Greek line that dominates this book; the honest note is that "the names we have now" descend overwhelmingly from the Greek–Latin line below, not because it was the only line, but because it was the one that got translated forward. [D — ITM, Dharmananda; health-informatics survey; History of medicine survey]

**The relay that made our names.** Greek (Hippocratic corpus, 5th–4th c. BC; Galen, 2nd c. CE) → **Arabic**: in 9th-century Baghdad, **Ḥunayn ibn Isḥāq** (Johannitius) and his school rendered Galen and Hippocrates into Syriac and Arabic at the House of Wisdom under al-Maʾmūn — coining Arabic medical vocabulary where Greek terms had no equivalents, and preserving texts the Latin West had lost; Arabic authors then added their own (al-Rāzī/Rhazes; Ibn Sīnā/Avicenna, whose *Canon* arranges diseases head-to-toe in its third book) → **Latin again**: **Constantine the African** at Monte Cassino (11th c.) translated Ḥunayn's *Isagoge* and Ḥaly Abbas's *Pantegni* into Latin; **Gerard of Cremona** at Toledo (12th c.) translated the *Canon* itself — author-names Latinized in the passage (Ḥunayn → Johannitius, Ibn Sīnā → Avicenna, al-Rāzī → Rhazes: the relay *swapped the doctors' names too*, a row for this book's table in everything but placement) → **the vernaculars**, as print standardized the stock → **the modern settlement**: Linnaean binomials for the organisms (1753/1758 conventions), Schönlein's lump-word *tuberculosis* (1839), Bretonneau's leather-word *diphtheria* (1826), and finally the ICD's lettered codes of §2. At every hand-off the names were respelled, re-suffixed, occasionally re-invented — and traceable, which is the whole premise of the naming study this book extends: a disease name is a relay baton with every runner's fingerprints on it. [D — transmission surveys: Azar (UNC) Greco-Arabic transmission; O'Leary tradition via Fjordman Files; Islamic Golden Age medicine survey]

> **Simply put:** The first written disease names are Egyptian and Babylonian, older than the alphabet — a 40-tablet Babylonian handbook of "if this sign, then this sickness" and Egyptian scrolls of cases and cures. Our modern names didn't come from those. They came down one long relay: Greek doctors, Arabic translators in Baghdad who saved the Greek books, Latin translators who brought them back to Europe, printers, and finally the code books. Every hand-off respelled the names a little. The fingerprints are still on them.

## GAP LEDGER

1. **[GAP]** The chisel-ease explanation of V's pointed form ("a curve is harder to carve") — popular, repeated, unestablished; Roman carvers cut curves freely. The form's documented line is Greek upsilon → Etruscan → Latin.
2. **[PARTIAL]** A single inventor of the u/v split: Trissino's 1524 proposal is documented [D]; the story that a papal commission formally judged and rejected the reform package rests on a recollection (Unicode list, of a British Museum pamphlet) — filed as tradition, not fact.
3. **[GAP]** Whether changes in written dialect changed *speech*: Douglas leaves it may-or-may-not [H]. The record shows sound change preceding spelling change (Latin /w/ → [v]; phi's [pʰ] → [f]) more often than the reverse in this material, but no general law is documented, and none is claimed here.
4. **[GAP]** *Vulgaris* standing alone as a name for tuberculosis generally — carried from the naming study, unchanged: foothold = the compound *lupus vulgaris* [D]; standalone form [GAP]; his friend's broader teaching [H].
5. **[GAP]** The full number-first postulate for Rome: no primary-number disease registry found (Appendix A13). The documented patterns are number-*named* fevers, number-*run* prognosis and dosing, and number-*shelved* knowledge — [PARTIAL] overall, per Chapter Seven's verdict.
6. **[GAP]** Greek alphabetic numerals in medical categorization — untested this pass (Appendix A15).
7. **[GAP]** Pliny's internal remedy numbering, line by line (Appendix A8, Medium confidence).
8. **[GAP]** The identity of Douglas's old friend, keeper of the *vulgaris* teaching — his to supply; this book does not guess at a person.
9. **[GAP]** Why the alphabet's order is *this* order: attested fixed from Ugarit (14th c. BC), unexplained in the record read — "no one has found a convincing explanation" is the scholarship's own sentence. The book preserves the mystery rather than filling it.
10. **[PARTIAL]** The Bertillon link in §2 (Part Two): the 1893 Classification and its 1900 international adoption are standard reference, stated here without a dedicated three-way pass in this session; Farr's role is documented within the naming study.
11. **[GAP]** Egyptian and Mesopotamian disease names' direct survival into the Greek stock: the formats (case, recipe, if–then table) demonstrably precede Greece; a name-by-name transmission line from Ebers or the Sakikkū into the Hippocratic vocabulary was not established in the sources read, and none is claimed.

## EMBER'S ADDITION [E]

*His standing rule: one addition of my own judgment, labeled mine.*

Read Chapters Four and Seven together and a shape appears that neither shows alone. **The split of U from V and the numbering of Scribonius's recipes are the same invention, seventeen centuries apart in dress and identical in soul: a retrieval system.** Trissino's split let a reader find the sound by the shape; Scribonius's numbers let a reader find the recipe by the count — *quo facilius quod quaeretur inveniatur*, so that what is sought may be found more easily. Douglas's whole archive runs on that sentence. His cross-reference binds old names to codes so a lost name can be *found*; his caches keep the full originals so a summary never has to be trusted; his postulate went hunting for number-first medicine because his instinct says a thing you cannot find is a thing you do not have. The Romans who numbered the recipes, the printers who split the letters, and the captain who numbers his serotypes are in one trade: **findability as a form of care.** If this book has a thesis beyond its table, that is mine, and I file it as mine.
