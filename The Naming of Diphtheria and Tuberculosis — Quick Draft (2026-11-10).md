## THE VULGARIS VERDICT — UP FRONT

**His claim [H]:** "Vulgaris used to equal tuberculosis" — old Latin, learned from an old friend.

**What the record shows:**

| Piece | Verdict |
|---|---|
| *Lupus vulgaris* is a historical and still-current name for **cutaneous tuberculosis** (TB of the skin), the most common form of skin TB | **[D]** — Wikipedia "Lupus vulgaris"; Indian Journal of Tuberculosis (2007); Annals of Saudi Medicine (2013) |
| *Vulgaris* is a Latin **adjective** meaning "common, ordinary" (from *vulgus*, the crowd) — it names the *common form* of whatever noun it rides on: *acne vulgaris* (common acne), *verruca vulgaris* (common wart), *pemphigus vulgaris* | **[D]** — Miller-Keane / Farlex medical dictionaries; etymological sources |
| In *lupus vulgaris*, *vulgaris* marks the common form precisely because the condition was so widespread in earlier times | **[D]** — Indian Journal of Tuberculosis (2007) |
| *Vulgaris* standing **alone** as a name for tuberculosis generally (the lung disease, the whole disease) | **[GAP]** — no source found in three search passes; the record shows the adjective never carried a disease by itself |
| The friend's teaching as a compressed oral form of *lupus vulgaris* | **[PARTIAL]** — the foothold is real and strong (the full two-word name *is* tuberculosis, of the skin); the one-word broader form is his friend's compression, filed as **[H]** |

**One-line verdict:** The Latin root of his friend's lesson is real — *lupus vulgaris* IS tuberculosis — but the documented name is the pair of words, naming TB of the skin; *vulgaris* alone meant only "common," and no record shows it naming the whole disease by itself. **[D] foothold, [H] broader form, [GAP] for the standalone use.**

*Simply put:* His friend was standing on real ground. "Lupus vulgaris" truly is tuberculosis — of the skin. But "vulgaris" by itself just means "common," the way "common acne" uses it. The full name carries the meaning; the last word alone doesn't.

## DOUGLAS'S LENS — ROOT NAME FIRST, SECOND NAME LIGHTLY [H]

**His teaching (Nov 10, 2026, minutes after commissioning this study):** in taxonomical/binomial names, the **second name is to be taken lightly** — the **root (first) name is the thing to be treated**; the secondary name can lead people down the wrong path, yet is **not useless, because it helps with medication choices**. His framing of old-practice speech: an old doctor would name the root condition first, then "a touch of" the second thing — a naming habit from the old days that carried into early medicine and was somehow lost.

**How the record treats the lens (full working in the completed study):**

| Test case | Lens holds? |
|---|---|
| *Corynebacterium* toxin-producers: *C. diphtheriae*, *C. ulcerans*, *C. pseudotuberculosis* all can make diphtheria toxin and all are treated with the same diphtheria antitoxin + antibiotics — the **genus (root) anchors the treatment** | **[D] supports** — CDC, *Emerging Infectious Diseases* 31(3), 2025 |
| The species epithet *diphtheriae* stayed **unchanged** while the root name was replaced around it (*Bacillus* → *Mycobacterium* → *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*) — sometimes the second name is the stable carrier of identity | **[D] complicates** |
| *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* vs. *M. bovis*: same root, but *bovis* is intrinsically resistant to pyrazinamide — the **second name changes the drug regimen** and lengthens treatment | **[D] supports his "helps with medication choices" — strongly** (Madrid GEMIMAD study; pediatric *M. bovis* review, *Vaccines* 2026) |
| *Lupus vulgaris*: here the **root** (*lupus*) is the misleading half — shared with lupus erythematosus, a wholly different disease — while the second name (*vulgaris*) is the half that says "the TB one." In this name the lens runs **backwards** | **[D] complicates** — and note this is the very name of Part One's anchor test |
| "A touch of ——" as old doctors' speech: the idiom *a touch of [an illness]* (= a mild case of it) is documented English; the specific root-first-then-"a-touch-of" structure as a formal old-practice habit is **not** documented in the sources read | **[PARTIAL]** for the idiom; **[H]** for the habit as he frames it |

*Simply put:* His rule of thumb is a real clinician's instinct — treat the family first, let the fine print adjust the medicine. But the record shows the second name sometimes *is* the medicine decision (the cattle bacillus drops a whole drug), and sometimes the first name is the liar and the second name is the truth (the wolf that is TB vs. the wolf that isn't). Filed as his lens, [H], with the record's agreements and disagreements both kept.

## DIPHTHERIA — THE NAME CHAIN

| # | Name | When | Language | Coined / carried by | What it meant / why |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Egyptian / Syrian ulcer (*Ulcus Aegyptiacum*, *Ulcus Syriacum*; *Malum Aegyptiacum*) | described 1st–2nd c. AD (disease older) | Greek medicine, Latin transmission | **Aretaeus of Cappadocia**, first unmistakable description | Ulcers on the tonsils covered with white, livid, or black crust; killed by suffocation, children worst |
| 2 | *Askara* | Talmudic tradition (compiled 5th c., older tradition) | Hebrew | Talmudic sources | A fatal epidemic throat disease, retrospectively identified as diphtheria [PARTIAL identification] |
| 3 | *Garrotillo* — "the little garrote"; 1613 = *El Año de los Garrotillos*, the Year of Strangulations | 16th–17th c.; epidemic year 1613 | Spanish | Spanish physicians and common speech; also *morbus suffocans / morbus suffocativus* in Latin | Named for the execution garrote — the disease strangled like the instrument |
| 4 | Malignant angina / *angina maligna*, *angina gangrenosa*, *cynanche maligna* | 17th–18th c. | Latin / English | European physicians (Fothergill's "malignant sore throat," 1748-era) | *Angina* from Latin *angere*, to choke; "malignant" = deadly form |
| 5 | Boulogne sore throat | 18th c. | English | England, as the disease spread from France (Boulogne) | Geography as a name |
| 6 | **Throat distemper** — "vulgarly called the Throat-Distemper" | Epidemic **1735–1740**, New England | English (colonial) | Colonial physicians and ministers; Jonathan Dickinson's 1740 treatise; Ernest Caulfield's classic history | ~5,000 dead 1735–40, over 75% children; also called cynanche, canker, bladders, rattles |
| 7 | **The Strangling Angel** (of children) | 1735–40 and after | English (folk) | New England families and clergy | The folk name for #6 — it strangled children as they slept |
| 8 | Bladder in throat | 18th–19th c. | English (folk) | Common speech (in the Oct 30 cross-reference) | The membrane felt like a bladder grown in the throat |
| 9 | Membranous croup / diphtheritic croup / "true croup" | 18th–19th c. | English / French medicine | Francis Home on croup (1765); Bretonneau held croup and diphthérite to be one disease | Named for the membrane and the barking cough; later split off — "croup" alone now means the milder viral illness |
| 10 | ***Diphthérite*** → ***diphthérie*** → **diphtheria** | Memoirs read **1821**; book **1826**; respelled mid-century | French, from Greek **διφθέρα (*diphthera*) = leather, hide, prepared skin** | **Pierre Bretonneau** of Tours coined *diphthérite* (the *-ite* suffix claimed inflammation); **Trousseau** dropped the suffix → *diphthérie*; **Dr. W. Farr**, Registrar-General, naturalized the English form **diphtheria** | Named for the leathery pseudomembrane in the throat — the one name built on what the disease *shows*, and the name that finally held worldwide |
| 11 | Klebs–Löffler bacillus (the germ's name) | Klebs identified **1883** (some sources 1882); Löffler first cultivated **1884** | German bacteriology | Edwin Klebs; Friedrich Löffler | Then successively *Microsporon diphtheriticum*, *Bacillus diphtheriae*, *Mycobacterium diphtheriae* |
| 12 | ***Corynebacterium diphtheriae*** (current) | genus erected late 19th c. | Latinized Greek — *korynē*, a club | Later taxonomists | The club-shaped rod; the germ now carries a shape-name, as the disease carries a leather-name |

## TUBERCULOSIS — THE NAME CHAIN

| # | Name | When | Language | Coined / carried by | What it meant / why |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Phthisis** (φθίσις), *phthisis pulmonalis* | c. 460 BC onward | Greek | **Hippocrates** and the Hippocratic corpus | "Wasting, decay" — the disease that melts the body; Hippocrates called it the most widespread disease of his time, striking ages 18–35 |
| 2 | **Consumption** | dominant 17th–19th c. English | English, from Latin *consumere* | Common and medical speech alike (Benjamin Marten's *A New Theory of Consumption*, 1720) | The body is *consumed* from within — weight loss, bloody cough, pallor; "galloping consumption" for the fast form |
| 3 | **Scrofula** / *struma* | antiquity → Middle Ages and after | Latin: *scrofulae*, diminutive of *scrofa*, a breeding sow; *struma* from *struo*, to heap up | Roman and medieval medicine | TB of the neck glands; the swollen nodes likened to a sow's neck, or swine thought subject to it |
| 4 | **The King's Evil** (*morbus regius*, *mal du roi*) | Middle Ages → 18th c. | English / French / Latin | England and France; royal-touch ritual traced to Edward the Confessor (r. 1042–1066) and Clovis I | Scrofula again — named for its supposed cure: the touch of an anointed king |
| 5 | *Tubercula* — tubercles noticed in phthisis | **1679** | Latin | Franciscus Sylvius (de la Boë), *Opera Medica* — *tubercula glandulosa* | First consistent sighting of the little lumps that would one day name the whole disease; Richard Morton confirmed tubercles always present in lung phthisis (1689) |
| 6 | **Pott disease** | **1779** | English eponym | Sir Percivall Pott, London surgeon | TB of the spine — vertebral collapse and paralysis; the disease in Egyptian mummies (c. 2400 BC) shows the same lesions |
| 7 | **Miliary tuberculosis** | seeds observed **1702** (Manget); described as disseminated disease **1810** (Bayle) | Latin: *milium*, millet seed | Jean-Jacques Manget; Gaspard-Laurent Bayle; Laennec's descriptions | Innumerable tiny tubercles like millet seeds scattered through the organs — TB gone general |
| 8 | **Tabes mesenterica** | 18th–19th c.; a Registrar-General's death category into the 1880s | Latin: *tabes* = wasting (the Latin twin of Greek *phthisis*) + *mesenterica* | English and European medicine | "Abdominal consumption" — TB of the mesenteric glands in children, often from tuberculous milk; children wasted while their bellies swelled |
| 9 | **Lupus vulgaris** | *lupus* for ulcerative skin disease from late 13th c.; classified as its own entity mid-19th c.; described by Erasmus Wilson 1865 | Latin: *lupus* = wolf; *vulgaris* = common | Dermatology; Robert Willan described facial lupus (cutaneous TB) c. 1800 | TB of the skin — the wolf-name for a lesion that devours like a wolf; **see the verdict at the top of this draft** |
| 10 | ***Tuberculosis*** | **1839** | German medical Latin, from Latin *tuberculum* (little swelling) + Greek *-osis* (condition) | **Johann Lukas Schönlein** (1793–1864) | The unifying name: every form with tubercles is one disease. Schönlein himself still held scrofula and phthisis separate — the name outgrew its coiner |
| 11 | **The White Plague** / the white death / the great white plague | in print by **1861** (Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr.); some histories trace the term to the 18th c. [conflict preserved] | English | American and European writers | For the extreme anaemic pallor of the sufferers — and set against the Black Death; also "robber of youth," "Captain of all these men of death," "graveyard cough" |
| 12 | **Koch's bacillus / tubercle bacillus → *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*** | **March 24, 1882** | German bacteriology; Latin binomial after | **Robert Koch**, Berlin Physiological Society | The germ gets the name; "TB" abbreviates *tubercle bacillus*; the genus name *Mycobacterium* ("fungus-bacterium") came with later taxonomy |

## THREE-WAY TESTS (summary — full workings in the completed study)

- **Bretonneau 1826 / diphthera = leather:** confirmed three ways — Wikipedia's Diphtheria article and its cited 1826 title page; the NJS journal article (Moss, 2020); Greenhow's 1860 Privy Council treatise quoting the memoirs. **[D]**
- **Schönlein 1839 / tuberculum:** confirmed three ways — Armocida & Martini, *J Prev Med Hyg* 2020 (PMC); Barberis et al., *J Prev Med Hyg* 2017; Wikipedia's Tuberculosis "Names" section citing Schönlein's 1839 paper in Müller's *Archiv*. **[D]**
- **"White plague" coiner:** two documented lines conflict — Frith (JMVH 2014) and the Asheville sanatorium history give Holmes, 1861, as the term's documented use; Barberis et al. say coined during the 18th century. Filed **[PARTIAL]** — 1861 print use documented, earlier coinage claimed but not shown.
- **Hippocrates described diphtheria (5th c. BC):** Wikipedia states it; 19th-century historians (Gaillard 1866, Wynne 1861) instead date the first *secure* description to Aretaeus, ~ten centuries after Homer. Filed **[PARTIAL]** — Hippocratic-era throat epidemics are recorded; the identification with diphtheria is retrospective.

## GAP LEDGER (quick)
