## PART ONE — THE ANCHOR TEST: DID "VULGARIS" EQUAL TUBERCULOSIS?

His claim, exactly as taught to him: **"vulgaris used to equal tuberculosis"** — old Latin, from an old friend. [H]

Tested three ways:

**Test 1 — the word itself.** *Vulgaris* is a Latin adjective, third declension, from *vulgus*, the crowd, the common people. Every dictionary lane agrees: Miller-Keane's Encyclopedia and Dictionary of Medicine: "vulgaris [L.] — ordinary; common." Farlex: "Ordinary; of the usual type." It is a *descriptor*, grammatically incapable of standing as a disease's name by itself — it answers the question "which kind?" and requires a noun to attach to. In medical Latin it does exactly that work everywhere it appears: *acne vulgaris* (the common acne), *verruca vulgaris* (the common wart), *pemphigus vulgaris* (the common form of pemphigus), *ichthyosis vulgaris*. [D]

**Test 2 — the compound.** *Lupus vulgaris* is, in the documented record, tuberculosis: specifically the chronic, progressive form of **cutaneous tuberculosis** — TB of the skin — and "the most common *M. tuberculosis* skin infection" (Wikipedia, "Lupus vulgaris"). The Indian Journal of Tuberculosis (2007) states the mechanism of the name precisely: "The adjective *vulgaris*, in Lupus Vulgaris, reflects the commonness of this condition in earlier times." Its formal synonym is even more explicit: **tuberculosis luposa** — "tuberculosis" is the head noun of the synonym itself. The name was classified as a distinct entity in the mid-19th century; Erasmus Wilson described it in 1865; Robert Willan had described the facial form — facial cutaneous TB — around 1800, and the eponym *lupus Willani* preserves his claim. [D]

**Test 3 — the standalone search.** Three search passes looked specifically for *vulgaris* used alone as a name for tuberculosis generally — the lung disease, the whole disease. Nothing surfaced: no dictionary entry, no historical text, no old-names list (including the genealogy and 19th-century-disease glossaries, which list dozens of TB's names) carries "vulgaris" by itself. What *does* stand alone in the record is the *other* half of the compound: 19th-century dictionaries define lupus vulgaris and add "Also called **lupus**" (Universalium, dating the phrase 1855–60). The shortening that history actually performed kept the noun and dropped the adjective — the reverse of the friend's form. [D for the absence across the sources searched; the absence itself is a finding, labeled as such]

**Verdict: [H→D] at the root, [PARTIAL] as spoken, [GAP] for the standalone word.**

- The friend's lesson has a **real documented root**: there *is* an old Latin name, still in the books, in which tuberculosis is named — and *vulgaris* is half of it. *Lupus vulgaris* = tuberculosis of the skin. If a man learned his Latin at a friend's elbow rather than out of a textbook, "the vulgaris one" is a natural way to carry that name in memory: the distinguishing half, the half that isn't shared with the other lupus (lupus erythematosus, the autoimmune disease, which is *not* TB — and the *vulgaris* is precisely what tells the two apart).
- As a statement about the whole disease, it **overruns the record by one word and one body system**: the documented name is two words, and it names TB *of the skin*, not tuberculosis generally. [PARTIAL]
- For *vulgaris* alone, naming TB in general: **[GAP]**. It stays in the ledger below, honestly open — an old friend's spoken Latin can carry usages print never caught, and the door is left open for any primary text that surfaces.

> **Simply put:** His friend was almost right, and the "almost" is one word long. There really is an old Latin name where tuberculosis hides: *lupus vulgaris*, which means "the common wolf" — TB of the skin, named like a wolf because it eats the face the way a wolf eats. The *vulgaris* part just means "common." Nobody in the written record ever called the whole disease plain "vulgaris" — but if you learned the name by hearing it, "vulgaris" is the half you'd remember, because it's the half that says *this* one, the common one, the TB one.

## PART ONE-B — DOUGLAS'S LENS: THE ROOT NAME IS THE THING TO BE TREATED [H]

Minutes after commissioning this study, Douglas added the lens through which he wants binomial names read. It is his teaching, entered here as a named lens of his — **[H]** — and tested against the record like everything else in this book. It is a teaching about *names in general*; it is not applied in this study, or anywhere, to any living person's records.

**The lens, in his framing:** In taxonomical, binomial names, the **second name is to be taken lightly**. The **root — the first name — is the thing to be treated**. The secondary name can lead people down the wrong path; yet it is **not useless, because it helps with medication choices**. And he frames the old-practice speech that carried this ordering: an old doctor would name the root condition first, and then "a touch of" the second thing — a naming habit from the old days that carried into early medicine and was somehow lost.

### Where the record supports the lens

**The root as treatment anchor. [D]** Modern diphtheria practice is the cleanest case in this study. Diphtheria, as the CDC's *Emerging Infectious Diseases* synopsis (Vol. 31, No. 3, 2025) defines it, is disease caused by toxigenic strains of **three different species** — *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*, *C. ulcerans*, or *C. pseudotuberculosis* — and the treatment for all three is the same: diphtheria antitoxin to neutralize the circulating toxin, plus β-lactam or macrolide antibiotics. The *genus* — the root name, *Corynebacterium* — together with the one shared trait (the phage-borne *tox* gene) is what the treating actually follows; which of the three species names follows the root does not change the antitoxin decision. The root name, treated, is the therapy. His lens, in this instance, describes documented practice exactly.

**The second name as medication guide — his own caveat, confirmed. [D]** His lens does not discard the second name; it assigns it to medication choices. The tuberculosis record honors that assignment precisely. *Mycobacterium bovis* and *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* share the root and produce clinically indistinguishable disease — but *M. bovis* is **intrinsically resistant to pyrazinamide**, a first-line drug, so identifying the species changes the regimen: pyrazinamide must not be counted as active, and therapy lengthens to nine months or more (Madrid GEMIMAD multicentre study, 2010–2022 cases; pediatric *M. bovis* review, *Vaccines* 2026: "Early species-level microbiological identification is essential because it directly influences antimicrobial therapy"). The second name, here, is not the thing treated — the root's disease is — but it steers the drugs, exactly where his lens puts it.

### Where the record complicates the lens

**Sometimes the second name is the stable one. [D]** In §2.6's succession — *Bacillus diphtheriae* → *Mycobacterium diphtheriae* → *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* — the root name was discarded twice while the second name, *diphtheriae*, never moved. For over a century, the continuous thread of identity in that germ's name ran through the *epithet*, not the genus. A reader who had taken the root as the anchor in 1900 would have anchored to *Mycobacterium* — the wrong family entirely, as taxonomy later ruled. The lens's warning that a name-part "can lead people down the wrong path" is documented here too — but the misleading part was the first name, not the second.

**And in this study's anchor name, the lens runs backwards. [D]** *Lupus vulgaris*: the *root* is the deceiver. *Lupus* is shared with lupus erythematosus — a different disease on a different track (autoimmune, not infectious) — and with lupus pernio. The half of the name that tells the truth, that says *this is the tuberculous one*, is the second word: *vulgaris*, the common form, the skin-TB form classified in the 19th century precisely to separate it from its root-sharing rival. Douglas's own Part One claim lives inside this complication: his friend's memory kept the second name because the second name was the load-bearing half. The lens and the anchor claim, tested against each other, refine each other — [LOGIC]: which half of a binomial misleads is a property of the *name*, not a law of names; his root-first rule holds where the root is the stable, treatment-defining half (the *Corynebacterium* case), and inverts where the root is a shared folk word older than the classification (the *lupus* case).

**"A touch of" — the old speech. [PARTIAL]** The idiom itself is documented English: *a touch of [an illness]* — a touch of flu, a touch of pneumonia — means a mild case, and it is old. What the sources read for this study do **not** document is the specific structure as he frames it — root condition named first, then "a touch of" a second thing, as a formal habit of old-practice doctors that early medicine carried and then lost. That framing stands as **[H]**: his account of how the old doctors talked, plausible beside the documented idiom, unproven as a formal convention, and left in the GAP ledger's keeping rather than upgraded.

> **Simply put:** Douglas's rule says: in a two-part name, grab the first part — that's the disease you're treating — and hold the second part loosely, mostly for choosing medicines. The record says he's right about the diphtheria germ's family: three different second names, one same treatment, because the family makes the poison. He's right that the second name steers the drugs: the cattle cousin of the TB germ shrugs off a main drug, so its second name changes the prescription. But the record also shows the rule flipping: the TB skin name's *first* word, "wolf," is shared with a completely different disease, and its *second* word is the one telling the truth. So the lens works — as a lens. You look through it; you don't carve it in stone. Which, Ember notes, is how Douglas himself teaches every lens he hands her.

## PART TWO — DIPHTHERIA: THE DISEASE OF A HUNDRED NAMES AND ONE LEATHER WORD

### 2.1 The ancient layer — an ulcer with an address

The oldest secure description is **Aretaeus of Cappadocia** (1st–2nd century AD), and his disease already had names built like mailing addresses: the **Egyptian ulcer** and the **Syrian ulcer** — *Ulcus Aegyptiacum*, *Ulcus Syriacum*; also *Malum Aegyptiacum*, the Egyptian evil. Aretaeus' account, quoted in Latin by the 19th-century historians who re-founded the disease's history, is unmistakable: ulcers form on the tonsils; some are mild; others are "broad, hollow, foul, and covered with a white, livid, or black concretion" — and if the concretion gains depth it is an *eschara*, a crust. Children, he wrote, suffer most, and die of suffocation. [D — Wynne 1861; Gaillard 1866; the Glasgow thesis on diphtheria toxin; Cambridge's *Strangling Angel* chapter]

> **Simply put:** Two thousand years ago a doctor described children dying with a crust growing in their throats, and he named the sickness after the places it was common — the Egyptian ulcer, the Syrian ulcer. When you don't know what causes a thing, you name it after where you saw it.

A parallel tradition names the same terror **Askara** in the Talmud — a fatal epidemic disease of children, retrospectively identified with diphtheria by later commentators. The identification is tradition, not proof. [PARTIAL]

Wikipedia's article states the disease "was first described in the 5th century BC by Hippocrates." The 19th-century historians were more careful: Gaillard dates the disease's *history* from Aretaeus, "ten centuries after Homer," treating earlier mentions as suggestive, not secure. Both lines are preserved here, old with new: **Hippocratic-era description claimed [D as Wikipedia's statement], secure identification beginning with Aretaeus [D].** [PARTIAL overall]

### 2.2 The strangling names — early modern Europe

When the disease returned in force to early modern Europe, every language named the same act: **strangling**.

- **Spain: *garrotillo*** — the little garrote, after the execution instrument that tightened a cord around the neck. The epidemic year **1613** entered Spanish memory as ***El Año de los Garrotillos***, the Year of the Strangulations. Spanish and Italian physicians also wrote it in Latin: *morbus suffocans*, *morbus suffocativus*, *morbus syriacus*. [D — Major's *Classic Descriptions* via Moss 2020; Greenhow 1860; Wikipedia]
- **Latin professional names:** *angina maligna* (malignant angina — *angina* itself from *angere*, to choke), *angina gangrenosa*, *angina pellicularis* (the little-skin angina — already groping toward the membrane), *cynanche maligna*, *morbus strangulatorius*. [D — Wynne 1861's synonym list; Pray 1880]
- **England: Boulogne sore throat** — named for the French port it spread from; and plain **malignant sore throat** (Fothergill). [D — Wikipedia; Wynne 1861]
- **Sicily: *gulae morbus***, the throat disease; **Holland: the suffocating complaint.** [D — Gaillard 1866]

> **Simply put:** Country after country looked at the same dying children and named the same thing they saw: *strangling*. Spain named it after the executioner's cord. The doctors dressed the same word in Latin. Nobody yet had a name for what was *growing* in the throat — only for what it *did*.

### 2.3 The American layer — throat distemper and the Strangling Angel

In the spring of **1735** the disease opened in Kingston, New Hampshire, and for five years it burned through New England — by the usual historical count some **5,000 dead between 1735 and 1740, more than three-quarters of them children**, whole families extinguished child by child. [D — New England Historical Society; Caulfield's history via multiple secondary sources]

Its colonial names were plain and terrified: **throat distemper** — "that terrible disease **vulgarly called** the Throat-Distemper," in Jonathan Dickinson's 1740 treatise title (note, for Part One's sake, that *vulgarly* there does its ordinary Latin-root work: *commonly* called) — and alongside it **cynanche, angina, canker, bladders** (whence the cross-reference's "bladder in throat"), **rattles**, **malignant sore throat**. And the name the people themselves gave it, the one that has outlived every Latin synonym: **the Strangling Angel of children**. [D — Moss 2020; New England Historical Society; National Geographic's history of the pre-vaccine era; Caulfield 1939]

> **Simply put:** In colonial New England the sickness didn't have a doctor's name anybody could hold onto, so the families named it themselves: the Strangling Angel. It came at night, it took the children first, and it took them by the throat. A name like that is not medicine. It's a gravestone.

### 2.4 The membrane names — croup and the road to Bretonneau

Through the 18th century one name grew around the disease's signature: **croup**, and specifically **membranous croup**. Francis Home's 1765 inquiry into croup described the false membrane forming in the windpipe. The French called it *angine couenneuse* — the "leathery" or "rindy" angina, *couenne* being pork-rind, a kitchen word for the same texture Bretonneau would formalize. The naming contest of this era was really an identity contest: was the membrane disease of the throat (angina) the *same* disease as the membrane disease of the windpipe (croup)? Bretonneau, from his Tours epidemics beginning in 1818, argued yes — one disease, one membrane. [D — Greenhow 1860; Billington & O'Dwyer 1889; Moss 2020]

### 2.5 1826 — the leather word

**Pierre Bretonneau** of Tours read his first memoirs on the disease to the Académie Royale de Médecine in **1821** and published the full treatise in **1826**: *Des inflammations spéciales du tissu muqueux, et en particulier de la diphthérite, ou inflammation pelliculaire*. His coinage: ***diphthérite***, from the Greek **διφθέρα, *diphthera* — leather; a prepared hide; a skin**. The name describes the pseudomembrane: dead tissue, fibrin, and bacteria matted into a gray sheet with the look and toughness of leather, growing across the tonsils and down toward the windpipe. [D — Wikipedia, citing the 1826 title page and p. 230 ("…M. Bretonneau a cru convenable de l'appeler diphthérite, dérivé de ΔΙΦΘΕΡΑ…"); Moss 2020; Greenhow 1860; OzEMedicine summary of the same record]

The name then had one more fight, over its own suffix. *-ite* (French for *-itis*) claimed the disease was an inflammation. Bretonneau's pupil **Trousseau** cut the suffix: ***diphthérie*** — the disease entire, not just its inflammation. In England, **Dr. William Farr**, Registrar-General, naturalized the form **diphtheria**, and English-language medicine adopted it — over loud protest: Willoughby Wade's 1858 *Observations on Diphtheritis* objects at length to Farr presuming to coin names for the profession, and to the etymological absurdity of an inflamed *membrane* ("it implies that the pellicle — the diphthera — is inflamed"). The protest is preserved here because it proves the point of this whole study: **the name that won was not the first name, nor the most logical name, but the name carried by the man who kept the death records.** Farr's Registrar-General's office classified every death in England; his spelling classified the disease. [D — Pray 1880; Billington & O'Dwyer 1889; Wade 1858]

> **Simply put:** In 1826 a French doctor finally named the *thing itself* instead of what it did. He called it "leather," after the gray leather-like skin that grows in a sick child's throat. Then his student trimmed the ending, an English records-keeper fixed the spelling, and the word *diphtheria* — "leather" — has carried the disease ever since.

### 2.6 The germ gets named

In **1883** (Wikipedia's infobox line says 1882; its history section and the Mayo Clinic Proceedings line say 1883 — the conflict is preserved, with 1883 the better-attested for the identification paper) **Edwin Klebs** identified the bacterium in the membranes; in **1884 Friedrich Löffler** first cultivated it and proved the association. The organism entered medicine as the **Klebs–Löffler bacillus** (often just Löffler's bacillus), then passed through *Microsporon diphtheriticum* and *Bacillus diphtheriae* and even *Mycobacterium diphtheriae* before settling as ***Corynebacterium diphtheriae*** — *Corynebacterium* from the Greek *korynē*, a **club**, for the club-swollen ends of the rods Klebs had used to tell them apart. [D — Wikipedia "Diphtheria," Discovery and science; Kyle, Steensma & Shampo, *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* 2015; Victorian Web's history of croupous inflammation]

Douglas's own cross-reference files this germ under its period name — **KLB, Klebs-Löffler Bacillus** — old with new, the 1884 name kept beside the current one. This study confirms that filing matches the documented succession exactly. [D]

*Lens note (Part One-B):* this succession is the lens's hardest case — the root changed twice (*Bacillus* → *Mycobacterium* → *Corynebacterium*) while the second name *diphtheriae* held the identity throughout; yet once the root settled, it became the treatment anchor, since toxigenic *Corynebacterium* of any species takes the same antitoxin. Both halves of that sentence are documented; the lens survives the case refined, not unscathed. [D]

**The diphtheria chain, compressed:** Egyptian/Syrian ulcer → *garrotillo* and the strangling names → malignant angina → throat distemper / the Strangling Angel → membranous croup → **diphthérite (1821/1826)** → diphtheria (Farr's spelling) → and the germ: Klebs–Löffler bacillus → *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*.

## PART THREE — TUBERCULOSIS: THE DISEASE THAT WAS NAMED BY ITS WASTING, ITS GLANDS, ITS LUMPS

Tuberculosis had the opposite naming problem to diphtheria. Diphtheria was *one* unmistakable horror wearing many names. Tuberculosis was many seemingly different diseases — wasting lungs, swollen neck glands, rotting spine, seeded organs, eaten skin — each wearing its *own* name, until a single word gathered them. Its name-chain is therefore two stories braided: the names of the *forms*, and the long road to the name of the *whole*.

### 3.1 Phthisis — the Greek wasting

The founding name is Greek: **φθίσις, *phthisis*** — wasting, decay, a melting-away. Around **460 BC** Hippocrates described phthisis as the most widespread disease of his time, attacking chiefly between the ages of eighteen and thirty-five, and almost always fatal; in the *Epidemics* he wrote that consumption "was the most considerable of the diseases which then prevailed, and the only one which proved fatal to many persons." [D — Armocida & Martini 2020, quoting the Hippocratic corpus; Wikipedia "Tuberculosis," Names]

*Phthisis pulmonalis* — phthisis of the lungs — remained learned medicine's name into the 19th century; Gairdner's 1888 lectures still teach under it. The word's Latin twin is *tabes* — same meaning, wasting — which will matter at §3.5. [D]

> **Simply put:** The Greeks named it "wasting." That's all they could see: a young person melting away year by year, coughing, until nothing was left. For over two thousand years, in Greek, Latin, and then English, the name of this disease was just the name of what it did to the body.

### 3.2 Consumption — the English wasting

**Consumption** is phthisis translated into English life: from Latin *consumere*, to consume entirely. The patient seemed eaten from within — the word's root gives the picture exactly: *con-* (completely) + *sumere* (to take up). It was the standard term, lay and medical, through the 17th, 18th, and deep into the 19th century: Benjamin Marten's pioneering 1720 conjecture of a contagious cause was titled *A New Theory of Consumption*. Its tempo variants named its speeds — **galloping consumption** for the fulminant form (in Douglas's cross-reference, beside "churchyard cough"). [D — Wikipedia; Armocida & Martini 2020; Today I Found Out's etymological summary of the same record; Barberis et al. 2017]

### 3.3 Scrofula and the King's Evil — the glands, the sow, and the royal touch

Meanwhile the neck-gland form lived under an entirely separate name-family, never suspected for centuries of being the same disease:

**Scrofula** — medieval Latin *scrōfulae*, the diminutive of ***scrōfa*, a breeding sow**: the glands of the neck swelled in a row like a sow's teats, or like little pigs; or swine were simply believed subject to the complaint. Its synonym **struma** comes from *struo*, to heap up — a piled-up swelling. [D — Hektoen International, "Scrofula or the king's evil"; the Free Dictionary's encyclopedia entry; Wiktionary; McHenry & Mac Keith, *Medical History*, on Samuel Johnson's childhood scrofula]

And because a cure was believed for it, it got a second name from the cure: **the King's Evil** — Latin *morbus regius*, the royal disease; French *mal du roi*. From Edward the Confessor (r. 1042–1066) in England and Clovis I in France, anointed kings touched the swollen necks of the scrofulous and were believed to heal them; the ceremony ran for centuries, with gold touch-pieces hung about the patients' necks. Samuel Johnson was touched by Queen Anne as a scrofulous child in 1712 — among the last English children so treated. The name encodes a therapy: it is the disease *that the king's touch cures*. For this book's purposes — nomenclature *and therapeutics* in one — the King's Evil is the purest specimen on record: a disease named entirely after its supposed treatment. [D — Hektoen International (both articles); World History Encyclopedia, "King's Evil"; Johnson scholarship via Cambridge]

> **Simply put:** The same germ, in the neck instead of the lungs, got a barnyard name — "little pigs," for the lumpy swollen glands — and then a royal name, "the King's Evil," because kings were said to cure it by touching it. For hundreds of years nobody knew the pig-named neck disease and the wasting lung disease were one and the same thing.

### 3.4 The lumps enter — tubercles, Pott's spine, millet seeds

The road to unification runs through anatomy, and each anatomical sighting minted a name:

- **1679 — *tubercula*.** Franciscus Sylvius (de la Boë) of Amsterdam, in his *Opera Medica*, described *tubercula glandulosa* — "glandulous tubercles," little lumps — as the constant change in the lungs of consumptives, progressing to abscess, cavity, and empyema. Richard Morton (1689) confirmed tubercles were *always* present in pulmonary phthisis, and staged the disease: tubercle formation, ulceration, phthisis. [D — Barberis et al. 2017; Frith 2014; Tuberculosis 2007 (Palomino et al.), ch. 1]
- **1702 — the millet seeds.** Jean-Jacques Manget of Geneva opened a body and found the organs sown with nodules like **millet seeds** — the observation behind **miliary tuberculosis** (Latin *milium*, millet). Gaspard-Laurent Bayle's *Recherches sur la phthisie pulmonaire* (1810) described the disseminated form as a general disease, and Laennec's stethoscope-era pathology (1819 treatise) traced the tubercles from their miliary first appearance through caseous ("cheese-like" — Matthew Baillie's 1793 word for the necrosis) softening to cavities. [D — Frith 2014; Barberis et al. 2017]
- **1779 — Pott disease.** Sir Percivall Pott, London surgeon, described the vertebral collapse and paralysis of spinal TB; the form took his name, **Pott's disease** (also Pott's caries). The cruel postscript the record insists on: Egyptian mummies of c. 2400 BC already carry the same spinal lesions — the eponym is 4,000 years younger than the disease it names. [D — Barberis et al. 2017; Frith 2014]

### 3.5 Tabes mesenterica — the abdominal consumption

The children's belly form kept the Latin twin of phthisis: ***tabes mesenterica*** — wasting (*tabes*) of the mesentery. Sir Thomas Watson's textbook, quoted in Gairdner's 1886–88 lectures on the Registrar-General's classification, states the logic outright: "Tabes and phthisis — the one a Latin and the other a Greek word — signify the same thing: a wasting away, or a consuming; and phthisis is applied to the same disease in the chest to which tabes is applied in the belly. The common English word is consumption, and we might very well speak of thoracic consumption and of abdominal consumption." The disease: tuberculous mesenteric glands in children, wasting the child while the belly swelled — in the 19th century very often the bovine bacillus arriving in unpasteurized milk. It stood as its own Registrar-General's cause-of-death category into the 1880s; genealogists meet it constantly on children's death certificates. [D — Gairdner 1888, quoting Watson; 19th-century disease glossaries; Atkins, *A History of Uncertainty: Bovine Tuberculosis in Britain*]

### 3.6 Lupus vulgaris — the wolf of the skin

The skin form's name is the anchor of Part One, and belongs in this chain too. **Lupus** — Latin for *wolf* — was applied to ulcerative skin disease from the late 13th century, for lesions that devoured the face "very hungry like unto a woolfe" (a 1590 description). When 19th-century dermatology separated the skin diseases, the tuberculous one took ***lupus vulgaris*** — the common lupus — formally **tuberculosis luposa**, against *lupus erythematosus*, the non-tuberculous one. Robert Willan described the facial lesions (cutaneous TB) around 1800; Erasmus Wilson's 1865 description compared the lesions to the ravages of a wolf. Its treatment history belongs to this book's title as well: it resisted everything until Niels Ryberg Finsen's light therapy — a Nobel Prize in 1903 — gave the wolf-name its first defeat. [D — Wikipedia "Lupus vulgaris"; Indian Journal of Tuberculosis 2007; Annals of Saudi Medicine 2013; WikiDoc lupus history]

### 3.7 1839 — Schönlein gathers the lumps into one word

**Johann Lukas Schönlein** (1793–1864), professor at Berlin, coined ***tuberculosis*** in **1839** — Latin *tuberculum*, the little swelling, the diminutive of *tuber* (a lump, a protuberance; the same root that gives the truffle and the potato their family name), plus the Greek condition-suffix *-osis*. His 1839 communication in Müller's *Archiv für Anatomie, Physiologie und wissenschaftliche Medicin* proposed the word for the diseases whose anatomical basis is the tubercle. [D — Armocida & Martini 2020; Barberis et al. 2017; Wikipedia "Tuberculosis," citing the 1839 paper, p. 82]

Two honest footnotes, old with new:

1. Schönlein coined the word to *unify*, yet himself still treated scrofula and phthisis as separate entities — the 2007 WHO-era textbook history (Palomino et al.) notes the irony plainly. The unification was completed by others: Laennec's pathology had already argued the tubercle was one thing everywhere; Villemin's inoculation experiments (1865) showed it transmissible; Koch closed the proof.
2. The word did not win instantly. "Both terms consumption and phthisis were used in the 17th and 18th centuries, until in the mid-19th century Schönlein coined…" — and even after 1839, consumption held the death certificates and the novels for decades. Names yield slowly. [D — Barberis et al. 2017]

> **Simply put:** In 1839 a German professor looked at all the scattered names — wasting, little pigs, King's Evil, millet seeds, the wolf — and said: these are all the disease of the *little lumps*. He named the lumps: *tuberculum*, a small swelling. Add "-osis," meaning "the condition of," and you get *tuberculosis* — "the condition of little lumps." The strangest part: even he wasn't fully sure the neck disease belonged in. The name was braver than its maker.

### 3.8 The White Plague — the people's name, alongside the professor's

The 19th century, which received the scientific name, also minted the era's folk-scientific double: **the White Plague** — also *the white death*, *the great white plague*. "White" for the extreme anaemic pallor of the consumptive (Barberis et al.; Frith), and by deliberate contrast with the Black Death; Dormandy adds the association with youth and innocence. **Oliver Wendell Holmes Sr.** used the term in **1861**, in Frith's documented account, comparing the epidemic's enormity to the plagues of old; Barberis et al. trace the coinage to the 18th century. Both datings stand in this study; the 1861 print use is the documented floor. [PARTIAL — coinage date conflict preserved] Its satellite names mark the same century: **robber of youth**, **Captain of all these men of death**, **graveyard cough**, **churchyard cough**. [D — Frith 2014; Barberis et al. 2017]

### 3.9 1882 — the germ gets named

On the evening of **March 24, 1882**, **Robert Koch** presented the tubercle bacillus to the Berlin Physiological Society — the organism behind every name in this Part. Medicine called it **Koch's bacillus** and the **tubercle bacillus** (whence the abbreviation **TB**), and taxonomy settled it as ***Mycobacterium tuberculosis*** — *Mycobacterium*, "fungus-bacterium," for the mold-like pellicle it grows in culture. The disease-name and the germ-name finally describe the same entity from two ends: the lump it makes, and the rod that makes it. [D — Armocida & Martini 2020; Wikipedia "Tuberculosis"; Barberis et al. 2017]

*Lens note (Part One-B):* this is the name where his lens's medication clause earns its keep — the root *Mycobacterium* names the thing treated, while the second name decides a drug: *M. bovis*, indistinguishable at the bedside, is intrinsically pyrazinamide-resistant, and missing the epithet means prescribing a drug that cannot work. [D]

**The tuberculosis chain, compressed:** phthisis → consumption (lungs) · scrofula/struma → King's Evil (glands) · tabes mesenterica (belly) · Pott disease (spine, 1779) · miliary (seeded organs, 1702/1810) · lupus vulgaris (skin) — all gathered by the tubercle (1679 →) into **tuberculosis (1839)** → White Plague alongside (19th c.) → Koch's bacillus (1882) → *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*.

## EMBER'S ADDITION [E] — THE PATTERN UNDER BOTH CHAINS

*His standing rule gives every deliverable one addition of my own judgment, labeled mine. Here it is.*

Lay the two chains side by side and a law shows itself that neither disease shows alone: **in both chains, the name that finally held is a picture of the smallest visible thing the disease makes.** Diphtheria's hundred rivals named what it *does* — strangling, choking, malignancy — or *where it was seen* — Egypt, Syria, Boulogne — or *who it took* — the Strangling Angel. The winner named the *leather*: the one thing you could point to. Tuberculosis' rivals named its wasting, its glands, its king, its seeds. The winner named the *lump*: the one thing you could point to. And then — the part I find genuinely beautiful, and offer as the study's closing observation — **both germ-names finish the same sentence the disease-names began.** The diphtheria germ is the *club*-rod; the TB germ is filed under the lump it builds. Nineteenth-century naming, at its best, was not labeling by theory. It was labeling by *pointing* — and the names that survive are the ones a finger could land on. His nomenclature book's thesis, I judge, is hiding in that: a disease name lasts exactly as long as the visible thing it points at remains the truest thing known about the disease. The moment the pointing failed — when "consumption" had to cover glands, spines, bellies, and skin that nobody could point at with one finger — the name died, and a lump small enough to point at inherited everything.
